## FAQs and Further Reading on 1 on 1 meetings:

Here’s a few of the questions asked during the webinar. Listen to the webinar for full answers. Below are brief responses and further reading:

### 1 on 1 meetings in small organizations:

Big companies have a number of advantages: high pay, clear career paths, and standard reporting structures/hierarchy. So what do you do if you’re leading or managing in a small organization? Here’s a few questions from the audience in the webinar.

**Q:** ***“My organization is relatively flat. It’s possible I could have 1-on-1’s with 15 people. At 1 hour per session, that’s 15 hours of my week, not very productive for me with operations. How do you feel about alternate week, 30-minute sessions?”***

A: Your best bet is to share the responsibility. Rather than doing a poor job with 15, get help so you and someone else can do awesome 1 on 1 meetings for everyone. That’s why the best practice is for teams no larger than 7 to 10 team members. [Read more on developing leaders as your team grows here.](https://getlighthouse.com/blog/developing-leaders-team-grows-big/)

**Q:** ***“What if I work in a very small company where career progression is limited. How can I help with career development?***

A: While promotions and moving up the job title ladder are good growth options, they’re not the only ones. Look for side projects you can get people involved in that they’re excited about, and help them grow within their existing roles. That’s just 2 of the many ways you can [grow your people without promotions (read more here).](https://getlighthouse.com/blog/employee-development-grow-cant-promote/) 

### What to do with your 1 on 1 meetings with great employees:

If you’re spending a lot of time [coaching up your underperformers](https://getlighthouse.com/blog/turn-around-disengaged-underperforming-employee/) or mentoring younger employees, you may overlook your stars. Here’s a couple of the challenges managers told us they had trouble with their best people.

**Q:** ***“I find it difficult to have fulfilling 1:1 with high performers every week as they are generally on top of things and don’t have enough items to go deep into for the full 60/90 minutes.”***

A: As Peter Drucker said, *“starve problems and feed opportunities.”* Your best people are opportunities. Use your 1 on 1 meetings to [help them continue growing](https://getlighthouse.com/blog/how-team-achieve-goals/). If they keep knocking out challenges then you can always channel more of their drive into big goals. Get their feedback and buy in on big changes, too. They’re likely to have insights that can make much of your efforts more successful. 

**Q: “*Any tips on how to handle 1:1’s with someone who is way smarter than you are?”***

A: It can be intimidating to manage someone that’s super sharp and more knowledgeable than you in a key area. Be humble. Don’t let your ego stop you from have great 1 on 1 meetings with them. Bring good questions to your 1 on 1s and let them teach you a few things. They’ll be grateful you’re willing to listen to their ideas. These [questions for 1 on 1s with individual contributors](https://jasonevanish.com/2014/05/29/101-questions-to-ask-in-1-on-1s/) and these [questions for 1 on 1s with a manager](https://getlighthouse.com/blog/questions-ask-one-on-ones-manager/) can help you spark great discussions.

### When 1 on 1 meetings are really challenging…

All your 1 on 1s can be awkward when you start them. They usually get better as you build rapport and trust with them. For those that don’t, or that have gone off the rails, they can be very challenging. Here’s a few challenges managers faced:

**Q:** ***“How do you handle situations when your employees don’t seem to want to open up and give you an opportunity to build rapport but rather take a standoffish approach to the relationship? Also, how do you combat the lasting impact of first impressions that might be negative from the employees?”***

A: Humility and persistence are a manager’s best friends. If you started on the wrong foot with them, confront it. You can often diffuse a situation by starting out by apologizing. It then makes it safe for them to admit mistakes, too. This post gives you a [step by step guide to turn things around if your team doesn’t like you anymore.](https://getlighthouse.com/blog/bad-leader-turn-around-unhappy-team/)

**Q:** ***“How do you handle one-on-one’s with a combative employee?”***

A: If any employee is combative, there’s probably a good reason. Try to find out why they’re upset and try to help make the situation better. Are they frustrated by something? Are they made at you? Whatever it is, show them some empathy and really listen. Even small changes can make them feel better and thaw a frosty relationship.

### Unique and special circumstances:

***Q: “How do you handle being authentic about focusing on long term career goals in 1:1s when your reports know you will not be in role for very long (likely to move to another role at your company before your reports move on)?”***

A: The best thing you can do as a leader is make the most of the time that you have. You may not be able to guide their career for the next 5 years, but you can help them grow while you are there: Buy them a book, get them involved on a project that will help them learn/grow, introduce them to someone that can mentor them. Even [small efforts can have a big impact](https://getlighthouse.com/blog/personal-goals-secret-happiness-team/) for people that have a lasting impact no matter how brief your time managing them.

**Q: “*Any specific areas you would recommend to cover in 1-on-1s with product managers who report to you… as they have influence on a much larger part of the org.”***

A: As a former product manager, I love this question! [Great product managers have peer one on ones](https://jasonevanish.com/2015/09/24/product-managers-peer-one-on-ones/) with many of their colleagues they work with. If you manage a product manager, encourage them to have them. Then, use your one on one meetings to find out the other things they’re learning in those meetings. You may be surprised the insights on morale, productivity, and other issues they’ll have.
